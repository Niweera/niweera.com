the canon

You arrived carrying a phrase. The Institute gave it to you, and it worked; it opened doors. Before you use it on the last one, read where it came from — and read what the canon the Institute quotes actually says about it.

They teach that there is a first speech: one original tongue, innate to every being, the language thought is written in before it is spoiled by the accident of where you were born. Learn it, they say, and you remember what you are. They are not the first to teach this; the Theosophists taught a version of it. It is an old and beautiful and seductive idea, and the scripture they cite to support it says the opposite.

the refutation

In the Cūḷavagga (Vinaya II.139), two monks of brahmin upbringing came to the Buddha. They complained that his teaching was being spoiled by men reciting it each in his own dialect, and they asked leave to fix it in the privileged, metrical, high-status form — the one correct tongue. He refused.

na, bhikkhave, buddhavacanaṃ chandaso āropetabbaṃ

— the word is not to be forced into [privileged] metre.

anujānāmi, bhikkhave, sakāya niruttiyā buddhavacanaṃ pariyāpuṇituṃ

— I allow it to be mastered each in his own dialect.

There is no root tongue. The canon says so, in its own words, at the exact place the Institute points you to and trusts you will not read past the first line.

The Institute is not inventing it from nothing, and I will not pretend they are. A commentary, written centuries after the words it explains, read the Buddha's "each in his own dialect" as in the Teacher's own dialect — and named that one privileged tongue, the very first speech the cult now sells. The interpretation is contested; honest readers have argued it both ways for a hundred years, and you should read them. But notice the shape of the choice: you are asked to trust the footnote over the text, because the footnote flatters you. That is the same trade as the spelling — the easy reading, taken because it was offered.

the part that is yours now

And yet the phrase opened the doors, and you used it. You are holding a thing the text in front of you calls a mistake, and you reached for it because it was offered and because it worked — which is how every door in this has opened. No one made you. You reached for it unasked, and you do not get to give that back.

One correction remains. The smallest one, and the whole point. The phrase you were handed was given lazily. The long vowel was flattened to a short one. The nasal that closes it — the one the grammarians mark with a single dot set beneath the letter, the niggahīta — was let fall to a plain consonant. Restore them. Put the length back over the vowel. Put the dot back beneath the m. The corrected words are the real ones. They are the last key. The canon would tell you not to need them.

You read the warning. Keep going. Everyone does.
